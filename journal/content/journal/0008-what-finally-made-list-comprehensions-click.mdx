For a week I have been avoiding list comprehensions. Every time one appeared in someone else's code I mentally replaced it with a `for` loop and moved on. Today I stopped avoiding it.

What confused me was the order. In a normal loop I write the condition first and the result last. In a comprehension the result comes first, which felt like reading a sentence from the end.

```python
squares = [n * n for n in numbers if n % 2 == 0]
```

Then I read it out loud in the order I would have written the loop: for every number in numbers, if it is even, give me its square. It was not backwards at all. Same sentence — the answer has simply been moved to the front, the way a newspaper headline puts the outcome before the explanation.

I rewrote four of my old loops as comprehensions. Two of them got clearer. The other two got worse: three conditions stacked into a single line that I could not read a minute later. I put those back.

That is the real lesson today. A comprehension is not a better loop. It is a shorter way to say a simple thing, and a bad way to say a complicated one. The choice is about the reader, not about looking clever.
